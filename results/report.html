HybridMobility-Coop: who takes the car, and who takes the child to school?

A small, reproducible benchmark of household agents that share one car and the job of accompanying a child, facing unexpected morning events. It compares rule-based, LLM-based and hybrid decision engines, with and without a decision-time negotiation protocol, against a central coordinator and an oracle. A fourth engine lets the LLM only translate the free-text news into a structured fact and leaves the decisions to rules and the protocol.

What this measures

Coordination quality and cost, not realism

The research this builds on (Vu, Gaudou & Oberoi 2025; Bru, Oberoi & Gaudou 2026) evaluates whether generative agents behave like real Toulouse travellers. This prototype asks a narrower question: when members must share scarce resources and the news changes the situation, which engine coordinates best, how much does talking help, and what does the LLM cost? Regret is the household's executed generalized cost (minutes-equivalent) minus the oracle's. A high-regret plan can still be a perfectly human one; testing human-likeness needs survey data this prototype does not have.

Findings

What the experiment shows

  1. Talking fixes known conflicts, and rules are enough for that. With communication, rule agents cut regret from 74.2 to 31.7 (paired difference -42.5, CI -64.2 to -24.2) and halve execution failures (0.56 to 0.25 per morning). They then match an exhaustive central coordinator with the same information on 118 of 120 instances. An LLM is not needed to negotiate the car or the school run when the situation is known.
  2. The small LLM is a poor decision-maker, even where it should help. qwen2.5:3b agents have higher regret than rules on every kind of event (87.1 vs 31.7 overall; 105.4 vs 62.7 on text-only events). Their reasons often mention the news ("the car is not reliable") while the plan ignores it: after a text-only car warning, only 3 of 15 committed plans avoided the car. In negotiation they rarely concede, which caused 9 deadlocks resolved by the protocol's logged tie-break.
  3. Gating the LLM saves calls but not quality. The event-gated hybrid uses 38% fewer LLM calls than the always-LLM agent (292 vs 468) and stays on rules on almost all ordinary and official-event days (5 escalations out of 45 such mornings). On text-only events, though, it inherits the LLM's mistakes. Irrelevant news also triggers the gate and changed 8 of 36 plans for the worse (+32 cost).
  4. Letting the LLM read and the rules decide works best. When the LLM only translates the free-text news into a structured fact once per household, and rules plus the protocol decide, regret drops to 6.6 (CI 2.6 to 12.3). That is lower than the central coordinator (paired -24.8; better on 44 instances, worse on 2). It uses 0.62 LLM calls per morning (84% fewer than the always-LLM agent) and leaves plans unchanged for irrelevant news (0 of 36 changed).
  5. The interpreter generalises to wording it was not tuned on, mostly. After one prompt revision on the scenario sentences (75/75 correct), it scored 74/80 on held-out paraphrases, including 20/20 adversarial decoys that mention cars, schools or buses. Five of the six misses share one wording that requires converting a clock time ("be there by 07:15") into minutes.

Does the committed plan follow the news?

For each text-only event, a plan is counted as consistent when it does what the news implies: no more drivers than working cars, no car trip into the blocked district, nobody travelling to a cancelled activity, and the member with the earlier deadline arriving on time. Rules and the central coordinator cannot read the news, so their score is the base rate.

Limits

What this prototype does not show

  • Nothing about behavioural realism: there is no survey ground truth, and travel times and cost weights are invented and declared.
  • Five hand-made households, one morning each, no memory or habit formation across days.
  • The text-only events were designed by the author and reward reading text by construction; the ordinary, official and decoy conditions bound that bias.
  • Two small local models at temperature 0: each LLM row is one deterministic policy, not a distribution over generations.
  • The negotiation is typed (keep/concede, accept/decline + option ID), not free dialogue. That keeps outputs checkable but limits what persuasion can do.
  • No GAMA, OpenTripPlanner or GTFS integration: the decision and protocol layer is built so it could sit behind a GAMA-to-Python service boundary like the one in the 2026 platform, but that is future work.
